Israa Hashem

PhD Candidate in Economics  ·  Concordia University  ·  Montréal, QC

CV Job Market Paper israa.hashem@mail.concordia.ca

I am a PhD candidate in Economics at Concordia University and an applied microeconomist working in the economics of education and labor economics. I am on the 2026–27 academic job market.

My research focuses on how a child's position within the family matters for educational outcomes. I am particularly interested in sibling influences, birth order, and the intergenerational transmission of education. My job market paper studies whether having an older sister rather than an older brother affects second-born children's school achievement, using large-scale administrative data from British Columbia and its linkages to other data sources.

In related empirical work, I study intergenerational educational mobility and field-of-study choices among immigrant and Canadian-born families. I also have a theoretical project in market design on school-choice mechanisms.

Job Market Paper

Does Having an Older Sister Matter? Sibling Sex, School Achievement, and Parental Investment

Siblings share much of childhood, yet we know far less about how they shape each other's learning than about the role of parents, schools, or peers. I study whether having an older sister rather than an older brother affects second-born children's school achievement and how parents invest in their schooling. Using British Columbia school records linked to the Census to identify siblings, and exploiting the as-good-as-random sex of the first-born, I examine second-borns' Foundation Skills Assessment scores in reading, writing and numeracy in Grades 4 and 7, as well as their enrollment in French immersion. I also compare second-borns with their first-born siblings to ask whether having an older sister offsets part of the birth-order gap in achievement. Results are pending Statistics Canada disclosure review.

Working Papers

Intergenerational Educational Mobility and Field-of-Study Choice in Immigrant and Canadian-Born Families

Immigrant parents in Canada are highly educated and concentrated in STEM fields, but do their children follow the same paths? Using the Survey of Labour and Income Dynamics, I find strong persistence in degree attainment across generations, but weaker persistence for children of immigrant fathers, pointing to higher educational mobility among second-generation immigrants. First-generation immigrants sort clearly into STEM, yet this sorting does not carry over to the second generation, whose degree choices converge with those of the Canadian-born. These findings have implications for how immigration shapes Canada's skill composition over time.

When Does a Stable and Pareto-Efficient Matching Exist?

with Szilvia Pápai

We study when a matching market has an outcome that is both stable and Pareto efficient. The question arises naturally in school choice and other priority-based allocation problems, where stability and participant welfare are both central objectives. These objectives can diverge substantially: even the student-optimal stable matching may leave large welfare improvements available to students. We develop constructive procedures that use the market's preferences and priorities to determine exactly when stability and Pareto efficiency are compatible. Our main procedure builds the matching step by step and succeeds exactly when a stable and Pareto-efficient matching exists. The results are also relevant for markets using deferred-acceptance-type procedures, where they identify when the resulting matching is already Pareto efficient for the students.
