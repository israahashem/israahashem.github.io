Work in Progress

The Effect of School Starting Age on Academic Achievement: Evidence from British Columbia

This paper studies the causal effect of school entry age on student outcomes using administrative data on students from kindergarten through grade 12 in British Columbia, Canada. I exploit the provincial kindergarten entry cutoff date in a regression discontinuity design to identify the effect of starting school one year older. I find a positive effect on Foundation Skills Assessment (FSA) scores in grade 4, with the magnitude of the effect smaller at later grades. Work is ongoing to examine the longer-run effects of school entry age on high school graduation and post-secondary outcomes.

Transmission of Educational Outcomes: A Comparison Between Immigrant and Native Families in Canada

This paper examines how educational mobility and field-of-study preferences differ across Canadian immigrant and non-immigrant families. We use the Survey of Labour and Income Dynamics data, focusing on variables related to immigration status and education. First, we reexamine the educational mobility coefficients for the overall population and observe persistence in degree attainment across generations. However, this intergenerational persistence in education is weaker for immigrant fathers, suggesting higher educational mobility among second-generation immigrants on the paternal side. Second, the paper analyzes the preferences for the bachelor's degree major choice across immigrant groups. Our findings indicate clear sorting into STEM fields among first-generation immigrants; this sorting is not persistent in the second-generation group, on average. More generally, we observe strong convergence in degree choice between second-generation immigrants and non-immigrants across most disciplines. Our findings highlight the assimilation of second-generation immigrants into the educational preferences of the native population.

Pareto-Efficient Matching with Gradual Acceptances

with Szilvia Pápai (Concordia University)

We introduce and analyze the class of Gradual Acceptance (GA) matching rules within the context of one-sided matching with priorities. GA rules extend the Immediate Acceptance rule through a step-wise selection process, slowing down the matching to allow previously rejected applicants to apply to their next highest-ranked schools before these schools are assigned. We show that all GA rules are Pareto-Efficient and satisfy basic stability and incentive properties. We also identify a subclass of Secure GA rules with especially good stability and incentive properties, revealing that stability and incentives can be jointly improved using simple selection algorithms while maintaining full efficiency.
